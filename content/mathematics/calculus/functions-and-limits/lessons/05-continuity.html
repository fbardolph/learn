Continuity

“You can draw it without lifting your pen,” made precise. It’s also the reason “just plug in” worked for so many limits.

Three ways to lift your pen

You’ve already met every way a graph can break at a point: a hole (Lesson 1), a jump (Lesson 3) and a blow-up (Lesson 4). Continuity is simply “none of those happen here”.

A function $f$ is continuous at $a$ when three things are all true:

  1. $f(a)$ exists: there is a dot at $x = a$.
  2. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x)$ exists: the left and right sides head to the same height.
  3. They’re equal: $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = f(a)$. The dot sits where the curve was heading.

Tap through the cases below and watch which test fails at $x = 1$.

  • 1. $f(1)$ exists
  • 2. $\lim_{x\to1} f(x)$ exists
  • 3. limit $= f(1)$

Continuous at $a$ means $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = f(a)$. That one equation quietly demands all three: a dot, a limit, and the two agreeing.

$f(x) = \dfrac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ is not continuous at $x = 1$. Which test fails first?

Plugging in gives $\frac00$, so there’s no value at $x = 1$: test 1 fails. The limit is fine. For $x \ne 1$ it equals $x + 1$, so the limit is $2$. It’s the “Hole” case.

Patching a hole

Holes and moved dots are the mild kind. The limit exists, so you can repair the function by putting the dot back where the curve was heading. These are called removable discontinuities. For $\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}$, add the rule “and $f(1) = 2$”. Now all three tests pass.

Jumps and blow-ups can’t be patched. No single dot can sit at two heights, or at infinity.

What value should $f(3)$ have to make $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2-9}{x-3}$ continuous at $x = 3$?

Factor the top: $x^2 - 9 = (x-3)(x+3)$.

For $x \ne 3$: $\frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} = x + 3$.

So $\lim_{x\to3} f(x) = 3 + 3 = 6$. Set $f(3) = 6$ and the hole is filled.

Continuous for free

You don’t have to run the three tests on every function. These are continuous everywhere they’re defined:

  • polynomials, like $3x^2 - x + 7$: everywhere;
  • fractions of polynomials, like $\frac{x+1}{x^2-4}$: everywhere the bottom isn’t 0;
  • $\sqrt{x}$ for $x \ge 0$, plus $\sin x$, $\cos x$ and $2^x$: everywhere;
  • and anything built from these by adding, multiplying, dividing (bottom not 0) or plugging one into another.

That’s the payoff. When a limit was “just plug in” back in Lesson 2, you were using continuity: for a continuous function, $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = f(a)$ by definition. The only interesting limits are at the points where continuity fails, which is why we kept hunting for $\frac00$.

Where is $\dfrac{x+1}{x^2-4}$ discontinuous?

It breaks where the bottom is 0: $x^2 - 4 = 0$ gives $x^2 = 4$, so $x = 2$ or $x = -2$. At $x = -1$ the top is 0, which is fine: $\frac{0}{-3} = 0$.

Gluing two pieces together

A common exam move: a function given in two pieces, with an unknown to choose so the seam doesn’t break.

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 + k & x < 2 \\ 3x - 1 & x \ge 2 \end{cases}$$

Each piece is a polynomial, so the only possible break is the seam at $x = 2$. Tap through:

  1. Left limit uses the left piece: $\lim_{x\to2^-} (x^2 + k) = 2^2 + k = 4 + k$.

  2. Right limit uses the right piece: $\lim_{x\to2^+} (3x - 1) = 3(2) - 1 = 5$. That’s also $f(2)$, since $x = 2$ belongs to the right piece.

  3. Continuous means the sides meet: $4 + k = 5$.

  4. Subtract 4 from both sides: $k = 1$.

Find $k$ so this is continuous: $f(x) = \begin{cases} kx + 1 & x < 3 \\ x^2 & x \ge 3 \end{cases}$ (a decimal is fine)

Left limit: $k(3) + 1 = 3k + 1$. Right limit and $f(3)$: $3^2 = 9$.

Set equal: $3k + 1 = 9$. Subtract 1: $3k = 8$. Divide by 3: $k = \frac83 \approx 2.667$.

The intermediate value theorem

Here is why continuity matters beyond tidiness. At 6 a.m. it’s 4°C, at noon it’s 15°C. Temperature doesn’t teleport, so at some moment in between it was exactly 10°C. You can’t say when without more information, but you know it happened.

Intermediate value theorem (IVT): if $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, then $f$ hits every height between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ somewhere in between.

The best-known use is finding roots. Take $f(x) = x^3 - x - 1$:

  • $f(1) = 1 - 1 - 1 = -1$ (below zero)
  • $f(2) = 8 - 2 - 1 = 5$ (above zero)

It’s a polynomial, so it’s continuous, and 0 lies between $-1$ and $5$. So there’s a root between 1 and 2. To narrow it down, check the midpoint and keep whichever half still has a sign change. Each tap halves the window:

Ten taps pin the root to within $\frac{1}{1024}$: it’s about $1.3247$. Calculators find roots in much the same way.

$f(x) = \dfrac1x$ has $f(-1) = -1$ and $f(1) = 1$, yet $f(x)$ is never 0. Why doesn’t that break the IVT?

The IVT needs $f$ continuous on the whole interval $[-1, 1]$. $\frac1x$ isn’t: it blows up at $x = 0$, jumping from $-\infty$ on the left to $+\infty$ on the right. The graph gets from negative to positive by lifting the pen, so it never has to cross 0.

Check

$g(x) = \begin{cases} x + 1 & x < 0 \\ x - 1 & x \ge 0 \end{cases}$. At $x = 0$, $g$ has…

Left limit: $0 + 1 = 1$. Right limit: $0 - 1 = -1$. They disagree, so the two-sided limit doesn’t exist (test 2 fails). No choice of $g(0)$ can fix that, so it isn’t removable.

Halving for $f(x) = x^3 - x - 1$ on $[1, 2]$: $f(1.5) = 3.375 - 1.5 - 1 = 0.875$. The root is in…

$f(1) = -1 < 0$ and $f(1.5) = 0.875 > 0$, so the sign changes on $[1, 1.5]$, and the IVT puts a root there. On $[1.5, 2]$ both ends are positive, so there’s no guarantee.

Find $a$ so this is continuous at $x = 1$: $h(x) = \begin{cases} 2x + a & x < 1 \\ x^2 & x \ge 1 \end{cases}$

Left: $2(1) + a = 2 + a$. Right and $h(1)$: $1^2 = 1$.

Set equal: $2 + a = 1$. Subtract 2: $a = -1$.

Which does the IVT guarantee?

$x^5 + x - 1$ is a polynomial (continuous), with $f(0) = -1$ and $f(1) = 1$. Zero is in between, so there’s a root. The first breaks at $x = 2$, so the IVT doesn’t apply. The third is $1$ at $x=1$ and $9$ at $x=3$, so 0 isn’t between them.
