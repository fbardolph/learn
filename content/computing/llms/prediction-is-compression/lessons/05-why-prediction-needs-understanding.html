Why prediction needs understanding

You’ll watch a memorizing predictor hit a wall, and see why the only way past it is to learn how the world works.

The cheapest predictor: count

The simplest way to predict the next character is to remember what followed the last few characters in text you’ve read. After “q”, English almost always has “u”. After “th”, “e” is likely. A model that predicts from counts of the last $n$ characters is an n-gram model. Before neural networks, this was how language models worked.

Below is one, trained on about 2,000 characters of plain English (a short passage about a town, a bakery and a train). Pick how many characters of context it looks back at. It reports bits per character, the Lesson 4 loss, on two things:

  • Seen text: the passage it was trained on.
  • New text: the sentence in the box, which it has never seen. Edit it if you like.

Grey: seen text. Blue: new text. Shorter bar = fewer bits = better prediction.

Each character of the new text is shaded by how surprised the model was: darker red = more bits.

Step through the orders from 0 up to 6. Two things happen at once. On the seen text the bits keep falling: with 5 or 6 characters of context, the model has almost memorized the passage. On the new text the bits fall at first, then stall well above that, and with long enough contexts they can even get worse.

As the n-gram model looks back further, why does its loss on new text stop improving?

A 6-character context like “ock st” might appear once in the training passage, or not at all. Counts from one example say almost nothing about the next case. The model has memorized its training text, and memorization doesn’t carry over to text it hasn’t seen.

Memorizing vs. generalizing

That gap is the whole story of this lesson. Loss on seen text can be pushed down by memorization: storing what you saw. Loss on new text only goes down through generalization: learning something true about how the text is produced, which then applies to cases you never saw. A model that does well on its training data but badly on new data is overfitting.

LLMs are judged on new text, and they see most of their training text only about once. So memorization alone can’t get their loss down. The question is what kind of “something true” lowers it.

Here’s the clearest case. A stream of addition problems, two-digit plus two-digit, with the answer as the token to predict. Two predictors:

  • Memorizer stores every problem it has seen. On a repeat it’s certain (0 bits). On a new problem it has no idea, so it spreads its bet evenly over the 179 possible sums (20 to 198) and pays $\log_2 179 \approx 7.5$ bits.
  • Rule has learned to add. It’s certain every time: 0 bits.

There are $90 \times 90 = 8{,}100$ possible problems, so repeats are rare: even after 1,000 problems, about 94% were new when they arrived. The memorizer’s bill grows with every problem. The rule’s bill stays at zero; all it ever cost was the few lines it takes to describe adding.

When data keeps coming, the rule is the better compressor. Memorized answers cost bits forever; a rule costs its own description once. Pushing loss down on endless new text pushes a model toward the rules that produce the text.

Suppose the answers could be any of 128 values, equally likely as far as the memorizer knows. How many bits does it pay on a problem it has never seen?

Each of the 128 values gets probability $\tfrac{1}{128}$.

Surprise: $-\log_2 \tfrac{1}{128} = \log_2 128$.

$128 = 2^7$, so that’s $7$ bits.

Reading a physics paper, one token at a time

Now real text. Here’s a sentence a physics textbook might contain. Tap each dashed piece to see what a model needs to know to predict it.

A ball is dropped from a height of 20 . Ignoring air , it falls and faster and hits the after about seconds, moving at about metres per second.

Green border: easy. Plain: needs knowledge of the world. Red border: needs physics and arithmetic.

For the “2”, the model has to do what a student does:

  1. Distance fallen from rest: $h = \tfrac12 g t^2$, with $g \approx 9.8$ m/s².
  2. Solve for $t$: multiply both sides by 2, giving $2h = g t^2$. Divide by $g$: $t^2 = \dfrac{2h}{g}$. Take the square root: $t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2h}{g}}$.
  3. Plug in: $t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 20}{9.8}} = \sqrt{4.08} \approx 2.02$ s.

And for the “20”: speed after falling for $t$ seconds is $v = g t = 9.8 \times 2.02 \approx 19.8$ m/s, about 20.

An n-gram model has seen “about 2 seconds” and “about 5 seconds” and “about 10 seconds”, and can only spread its bet across them. A model that can do the calculation puts nearly all its probability on “2”. Change the height to 45 metres and the right answer becomes 3 seconds; the counting model is no wiser, while the calculating one gets it right again.

A different textbook sentence: “A ball dropped from 80 metres hits the ground after about __ seconds.” Which predictor gives the right number the most probability?

$t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 80}{9.8}} = \sqrt{16.3} \approx 4.04$, so about 4 seconds.

A memorizer only helps if it has seen this exact setup. The formula works for every height, including ones that never appeared in training.

Where the remaining loss lives

Grammar and common phrases are learned early, because they repeat constantly. Once a model has them, those tokens cost almost nothing. What’s left of the loss is concentrated in tokens like the “2”: names, numbers, dates, the result of a step of reasoning, who did it in a mystery novel.

So once grammar is learned, the only way to lower the loss much further is to get better at the facts and the reasoning. A model trained to keep lowering it is being trained, indirectly, to know things and to work things out.

A model spends 0.2 bits on each of 80 easy tokens and 4 bits on each of 16 hard ones. What fraction of its total bits go on the hard tokens? (Give a decimal.)

Easy: $80 \times 0.2 = 16$ bits.

Hard: $16 \times 4 = 64$ bits.

Total: $16 + 64 = 80$ bits. Fraction on hard tokens: $64 \div 80 = 0.8$.

Evidence from inside a model

Is there any sign that trained models actually build such rules inside, rather than just looking as if they do? Two clean experiments say yes, at least for small models on simple worlds.

Othello-GPT. Researchers trained a small GPT on nothing but lists of moves from the board game Othello (“E3, D3, C4, …”), with the goal of predicting a legal next move. It never saw a board. Then they trained a small separate network, a probe, to read the board position from the model’s internal numbers, and it could. When they edited those internal numbers to change one square, the model’s predicted moves changed to match the edited board. To predict moves well, it had built an internal picture of the board: a world model.

Grokking. Small transformers trained on clock arithmetic (addition that wraps around, like hours on a clock) first memorized their training examples: training loss near zero, new-example loss high, just like the n-gram model above. With much longer training, the loss on new examples suddenly dropped. When researchers took the network apart, they found it had replaced the lookup table with an algorithm built from sines and cosines, treating numbers as rotations around a circle.

Sources: Li et al., “Emergent World Representations” (2023); Power et al., “Grokking” (2022); Nanda et al., “Progress measures for grokking via mechanistic interpretability” (2023).

What did the probe show about Othello-GPT?

It only ever saw move lists. The board was something it built for itself, because tracking the board is the most efficient way to know which moves are legal. The editing experiment matters: it shows the model uses that internal board, not just that the information happens to be there.

What this does and doesn’t show

The argument is about pressure, not proof. Lowering loss on new text rewards whatever internal structure predicts best for its size. Where a real rule (adding, falling bodies, a game board) is the cheapest route, training pushes toward it. Where a shortcut works on the training data, the model may learn the shortcut instead, and fail when the shortcut breaks.

So a low loss tells you the model has captured a lot of the structure behind the text. It doesn’t tell you which parts are real rules and which are shortcuts. Lesson 6 takes up the big bet built on this argument, and the case against it.

Check

A model’s loss on its training text keeps falling, but its loss on new text has started to rise. What’s happening?

That’s the n-gram picture at high order: seen-text bits down, new-text bits up. Grokking is the reverse event later on, when loss on new text finally drops.

In the grokking experiments, what had changed inside the network when loss on new examples suddenly dropped?

Memorizing more can’t help on examples it never saw. The jump came when it computed the answer (with rotations built from sines and cosines) instead of looking it up.

A memorizer has seen 50 of 350 possible problems. A new problem is drawn at random from all 350. What’s the chance it’s one the memorizer has not seen? (Give a decimal to 2 places.)

Unseen problems: $350 - 50 = 300$.

Chance: $300 \div 350 = 0.857 \approx 0.86$.

So 86% of the time it pays full price. That’s why memorization stalls as soon as the space of possible inputs is bigger than the training data.

In your own words: why does training a model to predict the next token of physics papers push it to learn some physics?

Grammar is cheap and learned early, so most of the remaining loss sits on tokens like numerical results and conclusions. Those can’t be memorized, because new papers have new setups. The only way to put high probability on the right number for a setup you’ve never seen is to compute it, which means having something like the physics inside. Lowering the loss keeps rewarding that. (It’s pressure, not a guarantee: where a shortcut works on the training data, the model may learn the shortcut instead.)
