What a gold standard is

A pound was a fixed weight of gold. Follow that one rule and you get fixed exchange rates, gold shipped across oceans, and prices that rise and fall without anyone deciding.

Warm-up

You met Britain’s return to gold in A map of the century. That date anchors this whole topic.

After the wars against Napoleon, in what year could you once again hand a Bank of England note across the counter and get gold for it?

1821. Waterloo was 1815; Parliament voted to go back to gold in 1819; the Bank actually started paying out gold again in 1821. (1844 is the Bank Charter Act, which tied note issue to gold.) Below you’ll see why the Bank had stopped paying in the first place.

A pound is a weight

In 1817 the Royal Mint began striking a new coin, the sovereign. It was worth one pound, and it held 7.32 grams of pure gold (alloyed with a little copper to make it hard). That number is the whole idea. Under a gold standard, a country’s unit of money is defined as a fixed weight of gold, and the authorities promise two things:

  1. Convertibility: paper money can be swapped for gold at that fixed rate, on demand. A £5 Bank of England note was a promise to hand over five sovereigns.
  2. Free flow: anyone can melt coins, export gold, or bring gold to the Mint to be coined.

Britain drifted onto gold in the 1700s (Isaac Newton, as Master of the Mint, set the gold coin too high against silver in 1717, so silver coins vanished from circulation) and made it law in 1816. For most of the century it was nearly alone. The rest of the world mostly used silver, or both metals, until the 1870s, the story of lesson 4.

Why the Bank stopped paying

The promise had already been broken once. In February 1797, with Britain at war with revolutionary France and rumours of a French landing in Wales, people rushed to swap notes for gold. The Bank of England’s vaults were draining, so the government ordered it to stop paying out gold. This Restriction lasted through all the wars with Napoleon. Paper pounds kept circulating, but they slipped below their gold value, and prices rose.

That is why 1821 mattered. Going back to gold at the old weight, rather than a lighter one, was a statement: a pound would be worth the same 7.32 grams it had been before the war. The price was painful: prices had to fall back to fit, which hurt farmers and debtors. But for the next century, apart from brief crises, a British pound was as good as gold, and the world came to trust it.

A gold standard is a promise: this much paper = this much gold, on demand. It holds only as long as people believe it, and governments tend to suspend it in wars.

What did the Bank of England stop doing in 1797, and why?

A wartime run (fear of a French invasion) had people swapping notes for gold, so the government suspended convertibility: the Restriction of 1797–1821. Notes still circulated, but they were no longer a claim on gold you could cash.

Exchange rates fall out for free

Here is the clever bit. If a pound is 7.32 g of gold and an American dollar is 1.50 g, then a pound is worth $7.32 \div 1.50 \approx 4.87$ dollars. Nobody negotiates it. It falls out of the two definitions, and economists call it the mint par. Try any pair below. These are the gold contents each country set by law (France from 1803, Germany from 1873, the United States in practice from 1879).

A sovereign holds 7.322 g of gold; a German mark holds 0.3584 g. How many marks to the pound? (Two decimal places.)

Divide the gold in a pound by the gold in a mark: $$\frac{7.322}{0.3584} \approx 20.43$$ So £1 ≈ 20.43 marks. Check: $20.43 \times 0.3584 \approx 7.322$ g. If you got 0.049, you divided the other way round, which gives pounds per mark.

Why gold crossed the ocean

In practice a merchant in New York who owed money in London didn’t send gold. He bought a bill on London (lesson 2 of the map), a paper claim payable there. The price of those bills, the market exchange rate, moved up and down with supply and demand. But it couldn’t move far from par, because there was always another option: buy gold in New York at the fixed price, ship it, and get pounds for it at the fixed price in London.

Shipping wasn’t free: freight, insurance, and the interest lost while the gold was at sea came to roughly half a percent, a round illustrative figure here (the real cost varied with the season and the ship). So the rate stayed in a narrow band around par. Its edges are the gold points. Move the market rate and see what a sensible merchant does.

So under the gold standard, exchange rates between gold countries were fixed in all but tiny wobbles, for decades. A London investor buying American railroad bonds didn’t have to worry about the dollar falling. That is part of why so much money crossed borders before 1914.

The market price of £1 in New York jumps to $4.95, well above par. What happens?

Above the upper gold point, buying gold at the fixed price in New York and shipping it ($4.8665 plus ~0.5% ≈ $4.89 per pound’s worth) is cheaper than paying $4.95 for a bill. Demand for bills drops, and the rate falls back. The band is enforced by people chasing profit, not by law.

The automatic thermostat

Gold moving between countries did something bigger than keep exchange rates steady. In 1752 the Scottish philosopher David Hume described what happens next, a loop now called the price–specie flow mechanism (“specie” means coin):

  1. Say British prices are high. Foreign goods look cheap, so Britain imports more than it exports.
  2. It pays for the difference in gold, so gold flows out.
  3. Less gold means less money in Britain, so prices there fall. Abroad, more gold means prices rise.
  4. Now British goods are cheaper, exports recover, and the flow stops.

Step it through. Each “year”, the country with higher prices loses gold to the other in proportion to the gap. Prices here simply track the gold stock, which is the cartoon version of Hume.

In real life central banks helped the thermostat along. When gold was leaving, the Bank of England raised its interest rate, Bank Rate. Higher interest pulled money into London and squeezed borrowing at home, which pushed prices down faster. In the 1920s Keynes and others called this behaviour following the “rules of the game”: when you lose gold, tighten; when you gain it, loosen.

In Hume’s mechanism, why does a country that imports too much eventually stop?

Under gold there’s no devaluation: the pound’s gold weight is fixed. The adjustment has to come through prices. Gold leaves, money tightens, prices (and often wages and jobs) fall until exports pick up. Self-correcting, but the correction can hurt.

The bargain

Put the pieces together and the gold standard is a trade-off:

  • What you get: money that holds its value over decades, fixed exchange rates, and a signal to lenders that you won’t inflate your debts away. Countries “on gold” could borrow more cheaply in London.
  • What you give up: control. You can’t print money to fight a slump. The money supply depends on how much gold is mined. When gold was scarce relative to a growing world economy, as it was from the 1870s to the 1890s, prices had to fall everywhere: the deflation of the Long Depression.

Hold on to the deflation point. When Germany and the United States switched to gold in the early 1870s (lesson 4), they added to the demand for a limited metal just as the 1873 crash hit.

Check

Which pair of promises defines a gold standard?

Convertibility at a fixed rate plus free movement of gold. Paper money was everywhere; what mattered was that it could be cashed in.

A French franc held 0.2903 g of gold and a sovereign 7.322 g. How many francs to the pound? (Two decimal places.)

$$\frac{7.322}{0.2903} \approx 25.22$$ So £1 ≈ 25.22 francs, the rate that stood, with small wobbles, from the 1820s to 1914.

Gold is flowing out of London. Under the “rules of the game”, what does the Bank of England do?

Raise the rate: foreign money comes to earn the higher interest, and dearer credit pushes British prices down, so the gold stops leaving. Suspending convertibility was the emergency exit (1797), not the rule.

In two sentences: what did a country gain by joining the gold standard, and what did it give up?

It gained stable money, fixed exchange rates with other gold countries and cheaper borrowing, because lenders trusted it not to inflate. It gave up control of its money supply: it couldn’t print money in a slump, and when gold was scarce its prices had to fall.
