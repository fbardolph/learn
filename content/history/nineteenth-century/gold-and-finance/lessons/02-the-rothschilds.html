The Rothschilds

A coin dealer from the Frankfurt ghetto, five sons in five capitals, and the first truly international bank, built on trust, information and a refusal to let outsiders in.

Warm-up

From last lesson: a sovereign held 7.32 g of gold and a dollar 1.50 g. What was £1 worth in dollars at mint par? (Two decimal places.)

$$\frac{7.32}{1.50} = 4.88$$ with these rounded figures, or $4.8665 with the exact ones. Either is fine. Keep in mind what that fixed rate means: a pound in London and $4.87 in New York were as good as each other, give or take the cost of shipping gold.

Judengasse, Frankfurt

Mayer Amschel Rothschild was born in 1744 in the Judengasse, the walled Jewish street of Frankfurt. Jews there were locked in at night and on Sundays, barred from owning land and from most trades. He started as a dealer in rare coins and medals, and one of his best customers was Prince Wilhelm of Hesse-Kassel, among the richest rulers in Germany. Much of the prince’s fortune came from renting out his soldiers to Britain, including for the war against the American colonies.

Mayer Amschel became Wilhelm’s court agent, handling bills and investments. When Napoleon’s armies overran Hesse in 1806 and Wilhelm fled, the Rothschilds helped look after and invest his money, much of it in London. That was capital and a reputation for discretion that no ghetto trader had ever had.

He had five sons, and he sent them out:

Amschelstayed in Frankfurt
Nathanwent to Manchester in 1798 to buy cloth, then to London and banking
JamesParis, from 1811
SalomonVienna, from 1820
CarlNaples, from 1821

The family crest shows five arrows held in one fist. The partnership agreement of 1810, and Mayer Amschel’s will of 1812, kept the business strictly inside the male line. Daughters, sons-in-law and outsiders got no share and no look at the books. The brothers shared profits from one pooled capital, wrote to each other constantly, and married within the family: many of the next generation married their cousins, which kept the capital together.

Which five cities did the Rothschild brothers operate from?

Frankfurt (Amschel), London (Nathan), Paris (James), Vienna (Salomon), Naples (Carl). Not Berlin or Amsterdam: the family worked with other banks there.

Gold for Wellington

Nathan made his name in London in the last years of the war against Napoleon. In 1814 the British commissary-in-chief, John Herries, needed to get coin to Wellington’s army in Spain and southern France, and subsidies to Britain’s allies, without shipping gold across a war zone at ruinous cost. Nathan, working with his brothers on the Continent, bought up gold and bills across Europe and delivered money where it was needed, often by moving paper claims rather than metal.

Then there’s the famous story: that Nathan learned of the victory at Waterloo (June 1815) a day before anyone else, by courier or pigeon, and made a vast fortune on the London market. It was popularised in an 1846 French pamphlet attacking the family, and repeated for a century and a half after. Historians who have worked through the Rothschild archives find little to support it. If anything, the sudden peace caught the brothers holding gold bought for a long war, and they had to scramble. The real basis of their power was duller and more lasting.

What does the evidence say about Nathan Rothschild and Waterloo?

The legend spread from an 1846 pamphlet and is not supported by the family’s papers. What did happen: from 1814 Nathan and his brothers moved gold and credit across Europe for the British government. That is what made them indispensable.

Why five houses beat one

Here is the dull secret. Each day merchants and governments owed money from one city to another: a Paris firm owes a London one, Vienna owes Naples, and so on. If every payment meant shipping gold, each one would cost freight, insurance and time. But if the payments run through one family with a house in each city, most of them cancel out. London owes Paris, Paris owes London: settle the difference only. The houses kept accounts with each other and moved metal only when a city’s total was badly out of line.

Try a day’s business. Each arrow is a payment a client needs made from one city to another, in thousands of pounds.

    Net inside the family

    Add speed: the brothers ran their own couriers and wrote to each other in Yiddish in Hebrew script, which few clerks or spies could read. A price gap between London and Paris could be spotted and used before most rivals heard of it. And add trust: a bill signed by any Rothschild house was accepted in all the others.

    The Rothschilds’ edge wasn’t one lucky trade. It was a network: one family, one pool of capital, five financial centres, private information, and payments that cancelled out internally instead of moving gold.

    Why did a family with houses in five cities need to ship far less gold than five separate banks?

    Netting. If London owes Paris £100k and Paris owes London £80k, only £20k needs to travel. Across five cities and thousands of bills, that saved enormous sums and let them charge less than rivals.

    Selling governments to the public

    After 1815, governments across Europe needed to borrow, to pay off war debts or build roads, and the Rothschilds became their favourite underwriters: they bought a whole loan from a government at one price and resold the bonds to investors at a little more.

    Nathan’s neat trick came with a loan to Prussia in 1818: £5 million at 5%, with interest paid in pounds, in London. A British investor no longer had to worry about the Prussian currency or collect payments in Berlin. Prussia’s debt became as easy to hold as a British one. This became the standard way to sell foreign bonds in London, and one reason London became the place where the world borrowed.

    Money brought status. In 1822 the Austrian emperor made all five brothers barons. In 1825, during a banking panic, Nathan helped the Bank of England bring in gold from the Continent to keep paying out. Later the family financed railways (James, the line from Paris to the Belgian border; Salomon, the first big Austrian line), managed huge French loans to pay the 5-billion-franc indemnity to Germany in 1871–72, and lent the British government £4 million overnight in 1875 to buy the Suez Canal shares. Nathan’s son Lionel became the first Jewish MP to take his seat in the House of Commons, in 1858.

    They were also hated, by those who saw them as the power behind every throne, and in antisemitic attacks that used the family as a symbol. The Waterloo legend came out of exactly that kind of pamphlet.

    What was new about the 1818 Prussian loan Nathan sold in London?

    Paying interest in sterling in London made a foreign bond feel like a domestic one. Investors loved it, the format spread, and London became the world’s market for foreign borrowing.

    Check

    How did Mayer Amschel Rothschild first get serious capital and a reputation?

    The coin dealer became the prince’s agent. Looking after Wilhelm’s money after 1806 gave the family capital and a name for discretion.

    Why did the family keep partnership strictly to the male Rothschild line and marry within the family?

    Pooled capital only works if it doesn’t leak out through marriages and inheritances, and private information is only private if outsiders never see the books.

    Which of these did the Rothschilds actually do?

    Lionel’s firm lent Disraeli’s government £4 million in 1875 for the Khedive of Egypt’s Suez shares. They helped the Bank of England in 1825 but never ran it, and the Latin Monetary Union (1865) was a treaty between governments.

    In two or three sentences: what was the real source of the Rothschilds’ power, as opposed to the Waterloo legend?

    A trusted family network: one pooled capital and five houses in Europe’s main financial centres (Frankfurt, London, Paris, Vienna, Naples), with fast private communication. Payments could be netted between the houses instead of shipping gold, prices and news could be acted on quickly, and governments came to them to sell their loans to investors, such as the 1818 Prussian loan paid in sterling in London.
