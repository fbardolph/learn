Britain’s century

A country with about 2% of the world’s people made nearly a quarter of its manufactures and lent money to half the planet. Here is how, and why it matters for 1873.

Coal under, wages up

Why did the industrial revolution start in Britain and not in France, China or the Netherlands? One influential answer, from the economic historian Robert Allen, is about prices.

British workers were expensive by the standards of the time, after a century of trade had pushed wages up. Meanwhile British coal was cheap, lying near the surface and near water transport. So a machine that replaced workers with coal-burning power paid for itself in Britain long before it did anywhere else. A spinning jenny or a steam engine in low-wage, wood-burning India would have lost money.

Once British engineers had built those machines, they kept improving them, until by mid-century they were cheap enough to pay off everywhere. Then Britain was selling the machines too.

On Allen’s argument, what made mechanisation pay in Britain first?

Machines save labour and burn fuel. Where labour is dear and fuel is cheap, that trade is profitable early. Inventors and empire mattered too, but the price story explains where the incentive was strongest.

Using the chart: who had passed Britain by 1900?

The US was at about 24% in 1900 against Britain’s 19%. Germany passed Britain just before 1913. India’s share had fallen from about 18% in 1830 to under 2%.

Free trade: a winner’s policy

In 1846 Prime Minister Robert Peel split his own party to repeal the Corn Laws, the tariffs on imported grain that protected landowners. Richard Cobden’s Anti-Corn Law League had campaigned for years that cheap bread meant lower costs for factory owners and better lives for workers.

Free trade was a generous-sounding principle, but also a calculation. When your factories are the most efficient on earth, open markets favour you: you sell manufactures everywhere and buy food and raw materials cheaply in return. Countries trying to catch up, like Germany and the US later, often saw it the other way.

The City: the world’s banker

Industry made Britain rich. The City of London made it central.

After the Napoleonic wars Britain put the pound back on gold in 1821: a banknote could be swapped for a fixed weight of gold at the Bank of England. The Bank Charter Act of 1844 tied the Bank’s note issue to its gold reserves. Few currencies were as trusted, so the world started pricing trade in sterling.

The key instrument was the bill on London: a promise to pay a sum in London on a set date, usually 90 days out. A cotton buyer in Buenos Aires or a tea merchant in Shanghai could pay with one, once a respected London house had “accepted” it, putting its own name behind the promise. Those bills then traded in Lombard Street like cash.

Follow one shipment round the loop:

  1. Manchesterindustry
    A mill ships cotton cloth to a merchant in Buenos Aires.
  2. Buenos Airesmoney
    The merchant pays with a 90-day bill on London, accepted by a London merchant bank such as Barings.
  3. Lombard Streetmoney
    The mill doesn’t want to wait 90 days, so it sells (discounts) the bill to a London bank for slightly less than face value. The Bank of England’s interest rate sets how much less.
  4. The Citymoney
    British profits pile up looking for a home. Barings and others sell Argentine railway bonds to British savers.
  5. The Pampasempire
    British-financed, British-built railways carry Argentine wheat and beef to the ports, to feed British workers more cheaply.
  6. Back to Manchesterindustry
    Cheap food keeps wages and costs down; Argentina earns pounds to buy more cloth. The loop closes.

Industry, money and empire fed each other through London. When the loop ran, everyone grew. When lending outran the railways’ earnings, the bonds went bad, which is exactly the kind of bust 1873 was. (Barings itself nearly collapsed over Argentine loans in 1890.)

What made a “bill on London” as good as cash for a merchant in Shanghai?

The acceptance put a famous name behind the debt, and the pound was convertible into gold. That pairing, trust in the name and in the money, is why sterling became the world’s trading currency.

The Rothschilds

Nathan Mayer Rothschild arrived in England from Frankfurt’s Jewish quarter in 1798 as a cotton buyer in Manchester. Within a decade he was in London, dealing in bills, gold and government debt. During the war against Napoleon he organised the shipment of gold to pay Wellington’s armies and Britain’s allies, using a network his four brothers ran from Frankfurt, Paris, Vienna and Naples.

The famous tale, that he made a killing on early news of Waterloo, was spread decades later in a hostile French pamphlet and is mostly legend. The real advantage was structural: five houses in five capitals that trusted each other, could move money across borders faster than any government, and shared information by private courier.

That is why Ahamed’s 1873 puts the family near the centre. By mid-century the Rothschilds were lending to governments across Europe. A world economy run through London’s bill market and a handful of banking houses is fast and efficient, and a shock in one capital travels to the others within days.

Check

Why did free trade suit Britain especially well in the mid-1800s?

A leader gains most from open competition. The losers were British landowners (cheaper grain) and foreign manufacturers who couldn’t match British prices, which is why later industrialisers often chose tariffs.

What was the real source of the Rothschilds’ edge?

The Waterloo story is largely a later legend. The network was real: brothers in London, Paris, Frankfurt, Vienna and Naples who could settle payments with each other and share news faster than rivals.

Roughly what share of world manufacturing did Britain have at its peak, around 1880?

Bairoch’s estimate is about 23% in 1880, from a country with only 2–3% of the world’s people.

In two or three sentences: how could a railway bust in Argentina or America hurt a mill town in Lancashire?

British savings financed foreign railways through London bonds. When the railways couldn’t pay, British investors and banks took losses, credit in the City tightened and interest rates rose, and the foreign customers who had been buying British goods with borrowed money stopped buying. Orders at the mill fall, so the bust comes home.
