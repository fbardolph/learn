Avoir ou être ?

A second, slower pass at the past tense with être: three questions to ask every time, a sorting game, and a short story from the morning after storm Austen.

Three questions, in order

The passé composé with être trips almost everyone up the first time. The slips come in three kinds, and each one is answered by a question:

  1. Avoir or être? The usual slips go both ways: nous avons resté (should be être) and le vent est soufflé (should be avoir).
  2. Which form of it? The auxiliary matches the subject: tu t’est → tu t’es.
  3. Does the participle agree? With être, yes: des arbres sont tombé → tombés.

Today you'll take them one at a time.

1 · Which verbs take être?

Last time the rule was “movement or change of state”. That's a good hint, but it lets you down on marcher (to walk) or souffler (to blow), which are movement and still take avoir. The real rule is simpler: it's a short, fixed list. Learn it as a life story, in opposite pairs:

be born ↔ dienaître (né) ↔ mourir (mort)
arrive ↔ leavearriver ↔ partir
go in ↔ go outentrer ↔ sortir
go up ↔ go downmonter ↔ descendre
go ↔ comealler ↔ venir
stay ↔ fallrester ↔ tomber
pass ↔ go backpasser ↔ retourner

Add the family members built on them (revenir, devenir, intervenir, repartir, rentrer) and every reflexive verb (se lever, se dépêcher, se retrouver…). Everything else takes avoir.

The être verbs say where you end up: in, out, up, down, here, gone, born, dead, still here. Verbs that say how something moves (marcher, courir, nager, rouler, voyager, souffler) take avoir.

Sort them

Tap the auxiliary each verb takes in the passé composé.

Hier, nous ______ chez nous toute la journée. (rester)

sommes restés (restées if “we” are all women). Rester doesn't move at all, but it's on the list: it says where you ended up. Think of the pair rester ↔ tomber.

Ils ______ trois heures sous la pluie. (marcher)

ont marché. Walking is movement, but it describes how you move, not where you end up, and marcher isn't on the list. With avoir, no agreement: never marchés.

2 · Which form of être?

Exactly the present tense of être, chosen by the subject. Nothing new, but it's where the eye slips when a pronoun gets in the way.

jesuis parti(e)
tues parti(e)
il / elle / onest parti(e)
noussommes parti(e)s
vousêtes parti(e)(s)
ils / ellessont parti(e)s

Est belongs to il / elle / on only. After tu it's always es, even with a pronoun in front: tu t’es levé. There is no t’est.

3 · Make it agree

With être, the participle behaves like an adjective describing the subject. Pick a subject and a verb and watch the ending:

Subject

Verb

Look back at the subject: feminine → +e, plural → +s, both → +es. Masculine singular → nothing. With avoir, never.

Les feuilles ______ pendant la nuit. (tomber)

sont tombées. La feuille (leaf) is feminine, and there are many of them: -e for feminine, -s for plural.

Mes parents ______ hier soir. (partir)

sont partis. A mixed group counts as masculine plural, so just -s.

Ma sœur ______ médecin. (devenir)

est devenue. Devenir is in the venir family, so être; ma sœur is feminine singular, so -e.

Reflexive verbs: four slots

A reflexive verb in the past has four pieces, always in this order: subject · pronoun · être · participle. Take the present, slip être in after the pronoun, and turn the verb into a participle:

tu te dépêchestu t’es dépêché(e)
elle se lèveelle s’est levée
nous nous couchonsnous nous sommes couché(e)s
ils se retrouventils se sont retrouvés

Listen for the difference. Elle se lève (“suh LEV”) is the present. Elle s’est levée (“SAY luh-VAY”) is the past. If you can't hear a “say” and a “-vay”, it isn't the past.

Je ______ tard ce matin. (se réveiller, passé composé)

me suis réveillé (réveillée for a woman). Subject je · pronoun me · suis · participle.

Tu ______ à quelle heure hier ? (se coucher, passé composé)

t’es couché (couchée to a woman). te + es → t’es: the tu form of être, not est.

Elles ______ devant la gare. (se retrouver, passé composé)

se sont retrouvées. se · sont · participle with -es for feminine plural.

L’histoire

Now all three questions at once, in a story. Before you tap a sentence, find each past verb and ask: avoir or être, and why?

Le lendemain de la tempête

Article d’entraînement écrit pour toi

Le vendredi 23 octobre, dans un village de la Manche, Hélène s’est réveillée à six heures.

On Friday 23 October, in a village in the Manche, Hélène woke up at six.

Il n’y avait plus de courant.

There was no more power.

Pendant la nuit, le vent a soufflé très fort, et le vieux chêne du jardin est tombé.

During the night the wind blew very hard, and the old oak in the garden came down.

Son fils Lucas s’est levé, il a regardé par la fenêtre et il a crié : « Maman, l’arbre ! La voiture ! »

Her son Lucas got up, looked out of the window and shouted: “Mum, the tree! The car!”

Ils sont sortis tout de suite.

They went outside right away.

Heureusement, l’arbre est tombé à côté de la voiture, pas dessus.

Luckily, the tree fell next to the car, not on top of it.

L’école a fermé pour la journée, alors Lucas est resté à la maison.

The school closed for the day, so Lucas stayed at home.

Les voisins sont venus avec une scie, et ensemble ils ont coupé le tronc.

The neighbours came over with a saw, and together they cut up the trunk.

À midi, Hélène et sa voisine sont allées en ville pour acheter du pain.

At midday, Hélène and her neighbour went into town to buy bread.

Le soir, ils ont dîné à la bougie.

In the evening they had dinner by candlelight.

À vingt-deux heures, le courant est enfin revenu, et toute la famille s’est couchée.

At ten p.m. the power finally came back, and the whole family went to bed.

What’s real: Storm Austen hit north-west France on 23 October 2026, bringing down trees across Brittany, Normandy and the north and leaving nearly 290,000 households without power (franceinfo). Hélène, Lucas, their village and their oak are invented.

What happened to the car?

L’arbre est tombé à côté de la voiture, pas dessus. À côté de = next to; dessus = on top.

Toute la famille s’est couchée. Why couchée with an -e, when the family has a son in it?

Agreement follows the grammar of the subject, not the people in it. La famille is feminine singular, so couchée, whoever is in the family.

Check

Which sentence is correct?

Tomber is on the list (rester ↔ tomber), so être. Le chêne is masculine singular, so no ending.

Les voisins ______ avec une scie. (venir)

sont venus. aller ↔ venir, so être; masculine plural, so -s. The participle of venir is venu.

Hélène ______ avant Lucas. (se lever, passé composé)

s’est levée. Four slots: Hélène · s’ · est · levée. Reflexive → être; feminine → -e. Not se lève, which is the present.

Translate: We stayed at home, and the wind blew all night.

Nous sommes restés à la maison, et le vent a soufflé toute la nuit. (On est restés is how most people would say it.) Question 1 gives you one of each: rester is on the list, souffler isn't. Question 3: restés agrees with nous; soufflé, with avoir, doesn't agree.
